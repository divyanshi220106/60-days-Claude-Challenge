Case File · Media Literacy Training

You're the fact-checker tonight.

Two pieces of content just crossed your desk — a headline and a social post. Nothing here is real, but everything about how it's built is. You'll read each one, make a snap judgment the way you would while scrolling, and then we'll pull back the curtain on exactly how it was engineered to work on you.

Why this matters

Most misleading media doesn't lie outright — it exaggerates, omits, and aims for your emotions instead of your judgment. Once you can name the technique, it stops working on you nearly as well.

Concept Briefing

Headline Detective

The idea

A headline's job is to get a click, and a body paragraph's job is to be accurate. When those two goals conflict, headlines often win — using loaded verbs, vague sourcing, or implied outrage that the actual reporting doesn't back up.

In everyday life: this is the gap between the headline you screenshot and share, and the three paragraphs down where the nuance actually lives.

Concept Briefing

Emotion Detector

The idea

Content that makes you feel something strong — fear, outrage, urgency, awe — gets shared faster than content that makes you think. That's not an accident. Specific word choices are often selected because they reliably trigger a specific feeling in a specific audience.

In everyday life: this is why the posts that make you angriest are often the ones you should verify most carefully before sharing.

What you learned

Three habits to carry with you

  • 01Read past the headline before you react or share — the exaggeration almost always lives in the first six words.
  • 02When something makes you feel a strong emotion fast, pause and ask who benefits from you feeling that way right now.
  • 03Check the claim against one independent source before repeating it — verification takes less time than the outrage did.
