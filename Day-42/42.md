# Day 42 — Personal Financial Command Center

## What I built

A single-file HTML/CSS/JS financial dashboard — **"The Ledger"** — generated by Claude through a guided interview instead of a static prompt. Claude asked MCQ-style questions first (who the dashboard is for, income situation, biggest concern, income range, debt status, savings habits) before writing a single line of code, then auto-designed the full app around my answers.

**My profile:** Student with a full-time job (₹25,000–₹50,000/month income), focused on tracking daily expenses and staying on budget, no clear debt picture, and just starting to save/invest.

## Modules included

- Overview dashboard with a Financial Health Score shown as a report-card grade (A+–F)
- Income & Expense tracking with category tagging
- Budget Planner with live progress bars per category
- Savings Goals tracker (Emergency Fund, New Laptop)
- Debt & Credit tracker
- Subscriptions tracker (a real leak for students)
- Cash Flow trend charts (6-month view)
- Investing Starter — SIP calculator with a growth chart
- What-If Simulator — sliders to project the impact of cutting spend or adding income
- Rule-based AI Insights generated from my actual data
- Printable monthly report
- Tips, a 30-day starter checklist, and AI literacy prompts

## Tech notes

- 100% vanilla HTML/CSS/JS — no chart libraries, no frameworks. Charts are hand-built SVG (donut, bar, line).
- All data persists to `localStorage` — nothing leaves the browser.
- Dark mode by default with a light mode toggle; fully responsive down to mobile with a collapsible sidebar/hamburger nav.
- `window.print()` produces a clean printable report using dedicated print CSS.

## Key learnings

1. **Interview-first prompting produces a much more tailored result than a single mega-prompt.** Answering 3 rounds of MCQs before generation meant the app was pre-seeded with a budget, categories, and a health-score formula that actually matched my situation — not generic placeholder categories.
2. **Constraining to "no external libraries"** forced hand-rolled SVG charts, which turned out lighter and more controllable than pulling in a full charting library for a single dashboard.
3. **A financial health score is only useful if it's explainable.** Breaking it into savings rate, budget adherence, debt ratio, and subscription load (rather than one opaque number) made the "why" visible.
4. **What-if simulators make budgeting feel active rather than punitive** — seeing "cut dining out 20% → +₹9,450 over a year" is a very different experience than a static warning.
